Property Tax Estimates: Rates, Bills, and the Spread Between States

Two numbers describe a property tax bill, and only one of them is printed on it. The nominal rate is local politics. The effective rate — tax actually paid divided by home value — is what an owner feels. On that measure, the same house carries a tax bill more than six times larger in the highest-rate states than in the lowest. This page uses the Tax Foundation's effective-rate ranking and the Census Bureau's bill data, and keeps the two measures separate.

Methodology

Included: Tax Foundation effective rates for 2024 (median tax paid divided by median owner-occupied value, the measure Tax Foundation author Janelle Fritz uses) and National Association of Home Builders analysis of Census Bureau American Community Survey bill data for 2023–2024. Excluded: county-level rates, which vary too widely within states to summarize honestly in a state table; nominal millage rates, which do not account for assessment ratios and exemptions; and any single-county assessor figure presented as a state figure. Prior-period figures are shown only where the same NAHB/ACS series published them.

What the numbers mean

The bill ranking and the rate ranking tell different stories, and the difference is the point. New Jersey tops both — the highest average bill ($9,767) and a joint-highest effective rate (1.88%). Hawaii sits at the opposite end on rate (0.29%) yet still produces substantial bills, because its home values are high enough to offset a low rate. A buyer who compares states on rate alone will underprice Hawaii and overprice low-value states; a buyer who compares on bills alone will miss why. The honest comparison needs both columns, which is why this page carries both.
