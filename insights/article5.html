Seven States, One Ballot: The 2026 Property Tax Revolt

Voters in seven states decide property tax measures on November 3, 2026, after the average single-family bill hit $4,427 and rose about 34% in a decade. The estimate that matters now is not last year’s tax — it is what the bill looks like after the ballots are counted.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

On November 4, 2025, a homeowner in Austin voted on two propositions and then did the math. Proposition 13 raised the school district homestead exemption from $100,000 to $140,000. Proposition 11 raised the additional exemption for homeowners 65 and older or disabled from $10,000 to $60,000, so a qualifying senior now has $200,000 shielded from school taxes. Both passed. A year later, on November 3, 2026, voters in seven states will decide measures of the same kind, and the Austin homeowner’s arithmetic is now the national arithmetic.

The pressure behind the ballots is measured in ATTOM’s 2025 property tax analysis: $396.8 billion levied on more than 89.6 million single-family homes in 2025, up 3.7% from 2024, with the average bill reaching $4,427, up 3%. The national effective rate rose from 0.86% to 0.90%, its highest since 2020, even as average estimated home values declined 1.7%. From 2016 to 2025, the average annual bill rose about 34%, climbing past $4,400. More than ten states have already passed some form of relief since the start of 2025. The November ballots are the second wave.

What the seven states are actually voting on

Florida has the most aggressive plan on the table. The legislature passed a homestead measure in a June 2026 special session, and Amendment 3 on the November ballot would raise the homestead exemption for all non-school levies to $150,000 on January 1, 2027 and $250,000 on January 1, 2028, indexed to inflation afterward, while the cap on annual assessment increases for non-homestead property drops from 10% to 5%. It needs 60% of voters to pass. Governor Ron DeSantis has pushed the plan as the centerpiece of his property tax agenda. A second Florida measure, Amendment 2, would exempt farm equipment and other tangible personal property used for agriculture or agritourism beginning in 2027.

Wyoming’s Initiative 1 would exempt 50% of the assessed value of a qualifying primary residence, on top of a 25% exemption already in effect. Oklahoma’s State Question 847 would tighten the caps on how fast taxable value can rise: the current caps of 5% for most locally assessed real property and 3% for homestead and agricultural land would fall to 4% and 1.75% beginning in 2027. Tennessee’s Amendment 2 would prohibit the legislature from imposing a statewide property tax at all. The remaining measures, in states including North Carolina, follow the same pattern: caps, exemptions, and prohibitions, each drafted to cut the bill without cutting the service in any way the voter can see.

The states that already acted show how fast relief reaches a bill. Indiana’s Senate Enrolled Act 1, signed in April 2025, created a supplemental homestead credit equal to 10% of the tax bill, up to $300, first appearing on spring 2026 bills. Texas files more protests than any other state, roughly 3 million in 2024 according to the state comptroller’s appraisal district data, and its voters answered with the two propositions above plus House Bill 1533, effective September 1, 2025, which reformed Appraisal Review Board training. Minnesota certified 2026 property tax levies of $13.78 billion in February 2026, up $873 million, or 6.8%, over 2025, a reminder that relief in one state is a levy increase in another.

The spread that drives the politics

The politics are driven by a spread that no national average captures. ATTOM’s 2025 analysis ranks Illinois first on effective rate at 1.84%, followed by New Jersey at 1.58% and Vermont at 1.40%, with Hawaii at 0.33%, Idaho at 0.39%, and Wyoming at 0.40% at the bottom. New Jersey’s average single-family bill reached $10,499, nearly ten times West Virginia’s $1,081, and Westchester County, New York averaged $18,386. The Tax Foundation’s analysis of Census Bureau survey data puts New Jersey first at about 2.23% to 2.47% and Illinois second at about 2.08%, a different ranking from a different method: ATTOM divides levied taxes by modeled market values, while the Tax Foundation divides taxes actually paid by median home values from survey data.

Property taxes accounted for 28.9% of total state and local tax collections and 70.0% of local tax collections nationally, according to the Tax Foundation’s 2026 analysis of fiscal year 2023 collections. That is why the revolt is so hard to satisfy: the tax being cut is the one that pays for the schools, the roads, and the fire department, and every exemption shifts the burden to someone else. A $300 credit in Indiana is paid for by someone. A $140,000 exemption in Texas is paid for by someone. The ballot never names who.

The counterargument: exemptions shift the burden, they do not cut it

The case against the revolt is straightforward, and it should be stated plainly. Exemptions and caps do not reduce the cost of government. They redistribute it. A homestead exemption shields the owner-occupier and raises the rate on everyone else, including renters, who pay the tax through their rent without ever seeing an exemption. A cap on assessment growth rewards the longtime owner and punishes the new buyer, whose assessment starts at market value while the neighbor’s is frozen a decade behind. Florida’s Amendment 3 would cut local budgets by billions if it passes, and the 60% threshold exists precisely because the legislature knows it.

There is also the timing problem. Relief that arrives by ballot takes years to reach a bill: Florida’s $150,000 exemption would not take effect until January 1, 2027, and the $250,000 step until 2028. A homeowner who budgets on the ballot promise and gets the 2026 bill instead has a cash-flow problem, not a tax problem. And the appeal system, which already favors the resourced, becomes more valuable as the stakes rise: the homeowner who can hire representation wins reductions, and the burden shifts to the neighbor who cannot.

But that argument, taken seriously, is an argument for better estimates, not for accepting the bill. If relief favors the informed, the remedy is to inform everyone, with the same comparable-sale, assessment-ratio, and exemption data the appeal firms already hold. The ballot measures change the math every year. An estimate that cannot say what a bill looks like after the exemption, after the cap, and after a successful appeal is a weather report for a flood: accurate, and useless.

That is the product: not a tax calculator, but a post-ballot estimate, one that tells a homeowner, a buyer, and a lender what the assessment will be, what the exemptions will shield, and what the saving is worth over the reassessment cycle.
