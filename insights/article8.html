Property Tax Rates by State: Where Homeowners Pay the Most in 2026

New Jersey and Illinois tie at a 1.88% effective rate. Hawaii sits at 0.29%. The sixfold spread is real — and the nominal rate still lies about the bill, because assessment ratios, caps, and exemptions decide what a homeowner actually pays.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

Take two identical houses, each worth $500,000. Put one in Essex County, New Jersey, and the other in Baldwin County, Alabama. At the Tax Foundation’s 2026 effective rates, the New Jersey house owes about $9,400 a year. The Alabama house owes about $1,850. Same house, same value, $7,550 apart, every year, for as long as either owner holds it. Over a 30-year mortgage that is more than $225,000 — the price of a second house, paid in tax, for the privilege of the same roof in a different state.

The ranking behind that arithmetic comes from the Tax Foundation’s 2026 table, built on 2024 Census Bureau American Community Survey data: New Jersey and Illinois tie for the highest effective property tax rate in the country at 1.88% of owner-occupied home value, followed by Connecticut at 1.54%, Vermont at 1.51%, and New Hampshire at 1.50%. Hawaii is lowest at 0.29%, then Alabama at 0.37%, Utah and Arizona at 0.48%, and South Carolina at 0.49%. The unweighted average across the 50 states is 0.90%, and the spread from top to bottom is more than sixfold. On a $300,000 home, the top rate means $5,640 a year and the bottom rate means $870. ATTOM’s 2025 analysis, which divides levied taxes by modeled market values instead of survey data, ranks the same leaders in a slightly different order — Illinois first at 1.84%, New Jersey second at 1.58%, Vermont at 1.40% — and the same laggards: Hawaii at 0.33%, Idaho at 0.39%, Wyoming at 0.40%. Two methods, one conclusion: where the house sits matters more than what it is worth.

The ranking, in dollars

Rates mislead when they are quoted without bills, so the bills deserve the lead. ATTOM’s 2025 analysis puts the average single-family tax bill at $4,427, up 3% from 2024, on an average home value of $494,231. New Jersey homeowners pay the most in dollars: an average of $10,499 a year. Connecticut follows at $8,901, New Hampshire at $8,174, Massachusetts at $7,904, and New York at $7,732. At the other end, West Virginia homeowners pay the least at $1,081, then Alabama at $1,284, Arkansas at $1,387, Mississippi at $1,563, and Louisiana at $1,639. Westchester County, New York, averages $18,386 — more than four times the national average, in a single county. The national effective rate rose to 0.90% in 2025 from 0.86% in 2024, its highest since 2020, even as average home values fell 1.7%. Bills rose while values fell, because the rate is set by local budgets, not by the market.

The two rankings disagree about New Jersey for a reason worth understanding. The Tax Foundation’s 1.88% divides taxes actually paid by median home values reported in a Census survey. ATTOM’s 1.58% divides taxes levied by market values from its own automated valuation model. Neither is wrong; they measure different things. Survey data captures what homeowners say they paid, including the effects of exemptions and caps. Modeled values capture what the assessor levied against an estimated market price. A state with generous exemptions looks cheaper in one and dearer in the other. The honest way to read any state ranking is to check which method produced it before quoting the number — and to remember that the statewide average is itself a fiction. No homeowner pays the state average. Every homeowner pays a county rate, on a county assessment, with county exemptions.

Why the nominal rate lies

The rate on the statute is rarely the rate on the bill. Three mechanisms stand between them, and each one moves real money. The first is the assessment ratio: the share of market value the assessor actually taxes. New York assesses at a fraction of market value and equalizes with a state-set ratio, so a 2% nominal rate on a fractional assessment is a far smaller effective rate than the same 2% on full value. Pennsylvania’s counties use a common level ratio that drifts from 100% as markets move, which is why two identical houses in neighboring counties can owe different taxes at the same millage. The effective rate — tax paid divided by market value — already accounts for all of this, which is why it is the only rate worth ranking. The millage on the tax bill is a local dialect. The effective rate is the translation.

The second mechanism is the cap. California’s Proposition 13, in force since 1978, holds annual assessed growth to 2% and taxes at roughly 1% of the purchase price, so a homeowner who bought in 1995 pays tax on a 1995 value while the neighbor who bought last year pays on today’s. Florida caps annual assessment growth on non-homestead property at 10% — and the November 2026 ballot would cut it to 5% while raising the homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. Texas reassesses every year, which makes its rates look high and its assessments honest at the same time. A cap does not cut the cost of government. It redistributes it, from the longtime owner to the new buyer, and the effective rate of a state with strict caps tells you less about a new buyer’s bill than about the average of thirty years of owners.

The third mechanism is the exemption, and it is the one homeowners control. Texas raised its school homestead exemption to $140,000 in 2025, with $200,000 shielded for seniors and disabled homeowners. Indiana’s 2025 law added a supplemental homestead credit of 10% of the bill, up to $300. Florida’s Amendment 3 would exempt the first $250,000 of a homestead from non-school levies. Every one of those carve-outs lowers the effective rate for the homeowner who claims it and raises it for the renter, the second-home owner, and the business that cannot. Property taxes supplied 28.9% of all state and local tax collections and 70.0% of local collections in fiscal year 2023, according to the Tax Foundation’s analysis, so every exemption is a transfer inside a system with no slack. The ranking tells you which states lean hardest on the tax. The exemptions tell you who inside each state actually pays it.

The counterargument: a low rate is not a low bill

Hawaii has the lowest effective rate in the country at 0.29%, and it is the worst example of what a low rate buys. The median Hawaiian home in ATTOM’s data runs about $747,545, so the lowest rate in America still produces a material bill — and a mortgage, an insurance premium, and a cost of living that dwarf the tax saving. West Virginia has the lowest average bill at $1,081, with an effective rate of 0.48%, and nobody moves to West Virginia for the tax climate alone. The high-rate states, for their part, are not simply expensive; they are states that fund their schools locally, without an income tax large enough to carry the load. New Hampshire has no broad-based income tax and the fifth-highest effective property rate. New Jersey’s $10,499 average bill pays for schools that rank among the country’s best-funded. A homeowner who moves from New Jersey to Florida for the property tax and discovers the insurance bill has not read the whole ledger.

There is also the federal footnote, which changed in 2025. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in July 2025, raised the cap on the state and local tax deduction to $40,000 for the 2025 tax year and $40,400 for 2026, up from $10,000. For a high-earning homeowner in a high-tax state, that quadruples the federal offset on a $10,499 New Jersey bill — real money, and a reason the sticker rate overstates the after-tax cost for exactly the households that pay the most. The deduction phases out at higher incomes, the cap is scheduled to revert, and renters get none of it, so the relief is partial and temporary. But a ranking that ignores it ranks the pre-tax bill, not the bill anyone files.

None of this makes the ranking useless. It makes it a starting point, not a verdict. A buyer pricing a $500,000 house in Illinois should budget $9,400 a year in tax, $783 a month inside the escrow payment, before the first showing — and should then check the county assessment, the exemptions, and the appeal history of the specific parcel, because the state average has never been anyone’s bill. The national average of $4,427 is about $369 a month, and the spread around it runs from $90 a month in West Virginia to $875 in New Jersey. The number that matters is not the rate. It is the estimate: the assessed value, the exemptions claimed, the cap in force, and the appeal that could cut it. That estimate, parcel by parcel, is the product.
