How Much Can a Property Tax Appeal Actually Save?

Nassau County published every grievance result for 2025: 224,983 homeowners won cuts worth $387.2 million. Texas county records tell a smaller, harder story. The gap between the two is the fee — and the filing rate.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

Nassau County, New York, keeps the most honest ledger in American property tax. Every year its Assessment Review Commission publishes the roll — every home, every grievance, every reduction — and the 2025 roll tells a story no appeal firm puts in its advertising. Of 391,629 residential properties, 224,983 won a reduction at the commission level. Together they saved $387.2 million in property taxes. The average winner cut $111,021 off the market value the county had assigned, about 9.5%. And 166,646 homes got nothing. Most of them never filed at all.

That last number is the one that matters. Nassau’s own review puts the appeal rate in large American municipalities near 2.9%. In Nassau, roughly 63% of homes filed a grievance in 2025. Ownwell’s 2025 survey of 2,500 homeowners found 74% worried about rising property taxes, and only 22% had ever appealed. The distance between worrying and filing is where the money sits: Ownwell counts $3.31 billion in potential Texas savings left unclaimed between 2023 and 2025. The average single-family bill reached $4,427 in 2025, according to ATTOM’s analysis of $396.8 billion levied on 89.6 million homes, and the national effective rate rose to 0.90%. Every unfiled appeal is a bill paid at the assessor’s number instead of the market’s.

What the county records actually show

Company marketing numbers deserve a cold read, and the county records give one. Ownwell’s 2025 study of appraisal district records across 11 Texas counties covered 54,804 successfully protested residential properties under $2 million. Its clients won 65% of protests; traditional agents won 57%. Net savings per successful protest, after fees, ran $120 for homes valued between $250,000 and $500,000, and $234 for homes between $1 million and $1.5 million, where Ownwell delivered roughly 1.4 times the market average of $162. In Dallas County, for homes in that top band, Ownwell won 64% of protests against a 53% competitor average. Those are modest numbers, and that is the point: a Texas protest typically saves hundreds of dollars, not thousands, because Texas reassesses every year and the disputed amount is one year’s tax on the disputed slice of value.

Nassau is the other end of the scale, because New York assessments compound differently and the bills are far larger. The 57% of Nassau homes that won reductions at the commission level saved $387.2 million in a single year, and the middle of the market did worst: homes between $500,000 and $750,000 won an average reduction of 7.4%, the lowest of any value band in the county, while North Hempstead homeowners did best at 11%. Ownwell’s own Nassau figures — a 93% win rate and $1,462 in average savings last year — sit above the county-wide averages, which is what a firm’s self-selected caseload should look like. The county roll, not the firm’s homepage, is the honest denominator: 57% of all homes won something, 43% did not, and the difference between the two groups was mostly a filed form.

Put the two jurisdictions together and the shape of an appeal’s value comes clear. On the average American home, a 9.5% assessment cut — Nassau’s average — is worth roughly $420 a year at the national effective rate. In Texas the typical successful protest nets $120 to $234 after fees. In Nassau it can run past $1,400. The saving scales with the bill, the bill scales with the rate and the value, and both vary more by county than by anything the homeowner does. A $4,427 average bill is a national abstraction. The saving is always local.

The fee decides what you keep

Most residential appeal firms work on contingency: no reduction, no fee, and a percentage of the first year’s saving when they win. The industry standard runs 25% to 50%. Ownwell charges 25% in Texas, New York, Illinois, and Washington, and 35% in Georgia, Florida, California, Colorado, and Pennsylvania. O’Connor & Associates, the Houston firm that has protested Texas values for decades, charges about 50% of the appealed year’s saving. Texas Protax charges 40%, with a $50 minimum per parcel. Flat fees run $150 to $500 regardless of outcome, and a real estate attorney bills $200 to $500 an hour or takes 25% to 40% on contingency. Filing yourself costs nothing but time — five to 20 hours of research, paperwork, and a hearing, by Ownwell’s own comparison — and in Nassau the filing itself is free, with only a $30 fee if a denied grievance goes to Small Claims Assessment Review.

Run Ownwell’s own worked example and the fee stops being an abstraction. On an $840 annual saving, a 25% contingency costs $210 and leaves $630. At 40% it costs $336 and leaves $504. At 50% it costs $420 and leaves $420 — half the saving gone for the filing. The same arithmetic cuts the other way on small wins: a $200 saving at 50% leaves $100, and at a firm with a minimum fee it can leave less. Flat-fee tools invert the math. AppealMyTax charges $49 once for a pre-filled evidence pack the owner files; TaxFightBack charges $79 flat. On a $1,500 saving, the $49 tool leaves $1,451, the 25% firm leaves about $1,125, and the 50% firm leaves about $750. On a $150 saving, the flat fee eats a third of it and the 25% contingency costs $37.50. There is no cheapest option in general. There is only the cheapest option for the size of your saving.

The term that costs homeowners the most is the one few read: the recurring fee. Some contingency contracts take their percentage every year the reduction survives, so a $774 saving — Ownwell’s published 2025 average among customers who saved — costs $193.50 a year at 25% for as long as the firm stays enrolled and keeps winning. A reduction that survives three reassessment cycles is worth three years of savings, and a firm that clips a quarter of each year takes a quarter of the whole prize. A flat fee, paid once, never comes back. A do-it-yourself filing, which costs nothing, keeps all three years. The question is never just the rate. It is the rate, times the years, against the size of the win.

The counterargument: the average is a sales pitch

Read the headline numbers the way a buyer would read a prospectus. Ownwell reports that 88% of its 2025 customers won a reduction on at least one appeal, with average savings of $774. Both figures count only customers who saved on at least one property that year, and the 88% counts a customer with several properties as a success when any single protest lands. Neither figure predicts what happens on one house, and neither includes the customers who saved nothing. The county-record number — a 65% per-protest win rate in Texas — is the honest one, and it is lower, because firms take the cases they expect to win and the county counts every protest filed.

There is a second discount to apply. A reduction is a one-year number on a moving target. Texas reassesses annually, so next year’s value starts fresh and the saving has to be won again. In states with caps, the arithmetic changes shape: California’s Proposition 13 holds assessed growth to 2% a year, which limits how much a reduction can compound, and Florida’s ballot measures would cut the non-homestead assessment cap from 10% to 5%. And the largest savings cluster where assessments are most wrong — new buyers whose purchase price resets the value, renovated homes, the top value bands — not evenly across the street. The neighbor who appeals and saves $1,400 does not prove you will. It proves the system misprices some houses by a lot, and the only way to learn whether yours is one of them is to check the assessment against comparable sales.

None of that makes appealing a bad bet. It makes the advertised average a bad way to size one. The expected value of an appeal is the probability of winning, times the saving if you win, minus the fee — and all three terms are local. In Nassau, where 63% of homeowners file and 57% win, the probability is high and the saving is large. In a Texas county where the typical net win is $120, a 50% contingency firm leaves $60 and a Saturday morning of do-it-yourself filing keeps the whole $120. The rational move in both places is the same: price the appeal before you file it.

That is the product, and it is why the estimate matters more than the appeal. A homeowner who can see the assessed value, the comparable sales, and the fee on the saving — before spending a dollar or an afternoon — files when the math works and skips it when it does not.
