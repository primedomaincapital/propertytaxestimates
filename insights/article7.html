How Long Does a Property Tax Appeal Take?

Most appeals resolve in two to six months. Informal reviews settle in two to four weeks; California cases can run fourteen months. The wait is rarely the problem. The deadline is.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The notice arrives in April, the deadline is May 15, and the hearing lands somewhere in the summer. That is the Texas version, and it is the fastest in the country. In Los Angeles County the same dispute can still be open fourteen months later, with the homeowner paying the contested bill the whole time. The difference is not the strength of the case. It is the county’s backlog, the state’s calendar, and whether the homeowner filed before the deadline in the first place.

Most property tax appeals take two to six months from filing to decision, according to AppealDesk’s 2026 guide to the process. Informal reviews resolve in two to four weeks. Formal hearings before a board of review typically take two to six months. Escalation to a state tribunal can stretch to six to 18 months. The Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, which tracks the rules for all 50 states, shows why the range is so wide: reassessment cycles run from annual in Texas to triennial in Cook County, Illinois, and longer elsewhere, and every stage of an appeal inherits the calendar of the county running it. The hearing itself is the shortest part. A typical board hearing lasts 10 to 30 minutes, with the homeowner getting five to 15 minutes to present comparable sales and the assessor answering. In Texas, the formal Appraisal Review Board hearing is usually 15 minutes. Months of waiting buy a quarter of an hour.

Three stages, three clocks

The first stage is the informal review: a phone call, an email, or a brief meeting with an appraiser from the assessor’s office. No board, no record, no lawyers. It is also where most appeals end. In Texas, roughly 85% of protests settle at the informal stage without ever reaching a formal hearing, and in Cook County many reductions are granted at the assessor’s office before a case reaches the Board of Review. Two to four weeks is the usual span, and a factual error — wrong square footage, a lot size the county misrecorded, a condition problem the model never saw — can close in a single conversation. The informal review is the cheapest speed in the system, and it is the stage homeowners skip when they assume the process starts in a hearing room. It does not.

The second stage is the formal hearing, and this is where the county’s volume sets the clock. Low-volume counties schedule in four to eight weeks. High-volume counties — Harris County, Texas; Cook County, Illinois — take three to six months. California runs longest: one to two months for the informal stage and three to 12 months for the formal hearing, four to 14 months all in, with Los Angeles County backlogs stretching waits to six to 12 months on their own. Florida moves faster at two to four months end to end, New York at two to six, New Jersey and Georgia at two to five. Pennsylvania, where deadlines and boards vary by county, runs two to seven months. The pattern holds across every state: the hearing is short, the docket is long, and the docket belongs to the county.

The third stage is the one homeowners rarely plan for. Lose at the board and the case can go to a state tribunal or a court — Michigan’s Tax Tribunal, New Jersey’s Tax Court, New York’s Small Claims Assessment Review, where the filing fee in Nassau County is $30. That stage adds six to 18 months, and it changes the economics: attorney billing at $200 to $500 an hour, which Ownwell’s fee guide quotes as the standard range, starts to make sense only when the disputed saving is large enough to carry it. Most residential appeals never get there. The board decision is the practical end of the road, and the homeowner who treats it as a warm-up for court has mispriced the dispute.

The deadlines that forfeit the appeal entirely

The wait is forgiving. The deadline is not. New Jersey’s appeal deadline is April 1, fixed every year. Nevada’s is January 15. Iowa’s filing window runs April 2 to April 30. Michigan requires attendance at the March Board of Review to preserve the right to go to the Tax Tribunal by May 31. Kentucky gives homeowners a 13-day inspection period and one day after it closes. Louisiana opens the rolls for about 15 days in July and August, varying by parish. North Carolina’s deadline is the day the county Board of Equalization and Review adjourns, usually in April or May. Texas gives until May 15 or 30 days from the notice, whichever is later. Miss any of them and there is no late filing, no extension, and no appeal — the assessment stands for the year, and the homeowner pays it.

That is the real timeline discipline, and it starts before the appeal does. The assessment notice is the trigger, and in most states the clock runs from the mailing date, not from the day the homeowner opens the envelope. A homeowner who diaries the deadline the day the notice arrives, files even with incomplete evidence, and amends the filing as comparable sales come in, never loses a year to a calendar. A homeowner who waits to build the perfect case and files it a day late loses the whole year, however strong the comps were. The counties know this. Their calendars are published, their windows are short, and the forfeiture is automatic. Roughly 3 million Texans filed protests in 2024, according to the state comptroller’s appraisal district data, and every one of them filed inside the window. The ones who missed it are not in the data. They are in the tax roll, paying the assessed number.

There is a second deadline hidden inside the first: the reassessment cycle. In Cook County, properties are reassessed once every three years by township, so a missed appeal can lock an overvaluation in for the rest of the cycle. In Texas, annual reassessment means a missed year costs one year. In California, Proposition 13 caps annual assessed growth at 2%, so a reduction won this year compounds for as long as the owner holds the home — and a year lost to a missed deadline is a year of compounding lost with it. The cost of delay is not the wait. It is the year the assessment survives unchallenged.

The counterargument: a fast denial is worse than a slow win

Speed is the wrong thing to optimize. An informal review that settles in two weeks because the appraiser barely looked at the comps is not a victory; it is a denial with a short fuse, and the homeowner who accepts it has traded a winnable hearing for a quick answer. The 85% informal settlement rate in Texas cuts both ways: it shows the system resolves most disputes cheaply, and it shows the system is built to clear its docket, not to maximize any one homeowner’s reduction. A board hearing that takes five months to schedule gives the homeowner five months to gather the evidence that wins it — recent sales of genuinely comparable homes, photographs of condition problems, the assessor’s own record with its errors marked. The homeowner who files early and prepares slowly usually beats the homeowner who files late and rushes.

There is also the matter of what the wait costs. The contested tax is generally due while the appeal runs, and a reduction that arrives in month six is a refund or a credit, not a bill never sent. In Nassau County, a grievance can only lower or hold an assessment, never raise it, so the downside of filing is the time spent — but the money still leaves the homeowner’s account on schedule. An appeal that takes 14 months in California is 14 months of paying the number under dispute. That is an argument for filing at the informal stage, where the money moves fastest, and for treating the board hearing as the backup rather than the plan.

The honest answer to the question in the headline is a range with a warning attached: two to six months for most appeals, two to four weeks if the informal review settles it, up to 14 months in California and six to 18 months if it reaches a tribunal — and zero months, no appeal at all, if the deadline passes first. The homeowner who knows the deadline, files inside it, and walks into the informal review with comparable sales in hand has already done most of the work.
